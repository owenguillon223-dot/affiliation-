For a lot of couples, the hardest part of planning a micro wedding isn't the budget or the
venue — it's telling family, especially if there's an expectation of a bigger, more traditional
celebration. Handled early and directly, this conversation is usually far less dramatic than
people fear going in.

## Lead with the decision, not a justification

Explaining at length *why* you're choosing a small wedding often reads as defensive, even when
that's not the intent, and can invite more pushback than a simple, confident statement of the
decision itself. State it plainly, then be ready to answer questions rather than pre-emptively
justifying it.

## Have this conversation in person or by call, not by text

A decision this significant, delivered impersonally, is one of the most common ways this
conversation goes worse than it needed to. A short, warm phone call or in-person conversation
signals that this matters to you too — even though the outcome is one you've already decided.

## Separate "informing" from "asking permission"

Be clear with yourself before the conversation about which one this is. If the decision is made,
frame it as sharing news, not opening a debate — mixing the two often leads family members to
push back harder, assuming there's still room to change your mind.

## Offer a way for them to still feel included

If a larger celebration is planned afterward, mention it in this same conversation rather than
as an afterthought — knowing there's still a way to celebrate together often softens
disappointment about not being at the ceremony itself.

## Expect a range of reactions, and give people time

Not everyone will react well immediately, and that's often more about their own expectations and
disappointment than a lasting problem. Giving people a little space to process news that differs
from what they expected, rather than needing an immediately positive reaction, tends to lead to
better outcomes over time.
