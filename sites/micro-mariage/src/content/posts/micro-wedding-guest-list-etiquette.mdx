A small guest list raises etiquette questions that simply don't come up at a 150-guest wedding,
because there's no room for the usual defaults ("everyone gets a plus-one," "invite the whole
office"). Here's how to think through the ones that come up most.

## Plus-ones become a real budget and headcount decision

At a micro wedding, a blanket plus-one policy for every single guest can double your headcount
unpredictably. A common approach: extend plus-ones only to guests in serious, established
relationships (engaged, married, long-term partners), and skip the blanket policy for casual
dating situations — not as a judgment, just as a practical necessity when every seat counts.

## Coworkers and larger social circles

At this scale, inviting "the whole team" usually isn't feasible, and partial invitations from a
larger group can create more tension than not inviting any of them. If your workplace or social
circle is large, it's often easier to be consistently selective (only genuinely close friends
from that circle) than to invite some and not others from the same visible group.

## Kids: an all-or-nothing decision, mostly

A "no kids except family" policy is harder to enforce gracefully than a clean all-or-nothing
rule, because it invites case-by-case debate. Most etiquette guidance agrees: pick one policy and
apply it evenly, rather than making exceptions that guests will notice and compare.

## Officiants, photographers, and other vendors don't count against your guest total

It's easy to forget that anyone working your elopement or micro wedding (officiant, photographer,
sometimes a small vendor team) is present but isn't part of your guest experience in the same
way — worth remembering when a venue's stated capacity feels tighter than expected.

## When someone assumes they're invited and isn't

This is the most emotionally difficult etiquette situation at a small wedding, and it's best
handled with a direct, warm conversation rather than letting them find out from someone else or
from social media. See
[How to Tell Family You're Having a Micro Wedding](/blog/how-to-tell-family-you-are-having-a-micro-wedding)
for how to approach conversations like this.

## The underlying principle

Every etiquette question above has the same answer: decide the policy once, in advance, and
apply it consistently — inconsistency is what reads as unfair or hurtful, far more than the
policy itself, whatever it is.
