You know the task. You know it's not that hard. You have the time. And you still can't make
yourself start it. If that sounds familiar, the problem isn't motivation, and it isn't the
difficulty of the task — it's task initiation, a specific executive function that ADHD affects
somewhat independently of how much you "want" to do something.

## Why willpower doesn't fix this

Task initiation problems don't respond to wanting to start more, because wanting was never the
missing ingredient. This is why "just do it" advice consistently fails for ADHD brains on tasks
they're avoiding — the instruction targets motivation, but the gap is somewhere else entirely.

## What actually helps

### Shrink the first step until it's almost meaningless

Not "clean the kitchen" — "put one dish in the sink." The goal of a tiny first step isn't to
finish the task, it's to get past the initiation barrier. Momentum tends to carry you further
than the tiny step once you're already moving — but the tiny step is what makes moving possible
at all.

### Use a body double

Working alongside someone else — even silently, even on a call where you don't discuss the task
— measurably lowers the barrier to starting for a lot of ADHD brains. See
[Body Doubling Apps for ADHD](/blog/body-doubling-apps-for-adhd) for how to try this for free
before paying for anything.

### Set a timer for less time than you think you need

Committing to "just 5 minutes" is easier to start than committing to "finish the task," because
it removes the open-ended commitment that makes big tasks feel heavier than they are. Most
people who use this keep going past 5 minutes once they've started — but the deal you make with
yourself should genuinely be just 5 minutes, or the trick stops working.

### Change the environment, not just the intention

Move to a different room, work from a coffee shop, close every tab except the one you need.
Environmental cues matter more for task initiation than most people expect — the same task in
the same spot where you've failed to start it ten times carries an association that a fresh
environment doesn't have.

### Pair the task with something you don't dread

Music, a snack you like, a specific playlist reserved only for tasks you're avoiding — building
a small, consistent association between "starting the dreaded thing" and "something mildly
pleasant" can lower resistance over time, even though it doesn't touch the task itself.

## What doesn't help (and why it's worth naming)

Guilt, self-criticism, and "trying harder" don't address task initiation — they add an emotional
tax on top of an already-present executive function gap, which tends to make the next attempt
harder, not easier. If a strategy above doesn't work, the fix is to try a different strategy —
not to conclude you didn't try hard enough.
