Most advice about ADHD mornings treats the morning as an isolated 30-minute window you need to
fix. It isn't isolated — a workable morning is mostly decided the night before, supported by a
handful of self-regulation tools you can reach for when things go sideways anyway, because they
sometimes will. This guide ties those pieces together into one system.

## Start with the core principle: fewer decisions, not more discipline

Every piece of advice on this site about mornings comes back to the same idea: ADHD mornings
don't fail because of a lack of willpower, they fail because too many small decisions are packed
into a time of day when executive function is already stretched thin. The fix is structural —
remove decision points — not motivational.

Three structural moves do most of the work:

1. **Move decisions to the night before**, when there's more mental bandwidth to make them
2. **Make the routine visible**, outside your head, so it doesn't compete with every other
   thought for attention
3. **Build in a reset step**, so one skipped part doesn't collapse the whole morning

The full reasoning and a sample routine built on these three moves is in
[ADHD Morning Routine for People Who Hate Mornings](/blog/adhd-morning-routine-for-people-who-hate-mornings).

## The morning starts the night before

A good morning routine is downstream of a good night routine — clothes laid out, bag packed, and
tomorrow's first task written down all happen the evening before, when there's more attention
available to make those calls. The full checklist, including why evenings are structurally easier
to plan in than mornings, is in
[ADHD Night Routine Checklist](/blog/adhd-night-routine-checklist).

## When the routine isn't enough on its own

Even a well-designed routine doesn't remove the need for a tool during moments of overwhelm,
boredom, or dysregulation — a pre-decided list of go-to options, sorted by how much time and
energy you have, removes the need to invent a solution while you're already struggling to think
clearly. That's what a dopamine menu is for, and how to build one is covered in
[Dopamine Menu: What It Is + Free Printable](/blog/dopamine-menu-adhd-free-printable).

## Putting it together

A full system looks like this:

- **Evening:** run the night routine checklist — clothes, bag, tomorrow's first task, phone out
  of the bedroom
- **Morning:** run the visible morning checklist, anchored to something that already happens
  (after the alarm, after brushing your teeth) rather than to a specific time
- **Anytime things go sideways anyway:** reach for the dopamine menu instead of trying to
  generate a fix from scratch in the moment

None of these three pieces needs to be perfect on its own — the point of building them as a
connected system is that a rough morning doesn't have to mean a lost day, because there's always
a next, smaller step already decided in advance.

## Common mistakes across all three

- **Making any single piece too long.** A five-step checklist gets used; a fifteen-step one gets
  abandoned within a week, at any time of day.
- **Keeping the system in your head.** Every piece of this guide works because it's written down
  and visible — not because it's remembered.
- **Treating a skipped day as failure instead of data.** A missed morning routine is information
  about what needs adjusting, not evidence the system doesn't work.

Start with just the morning routine article if you want the smallest possible first step —
everything else here can be added once that much is running on its own.
