This isn't a medical article, and it isn't an argument against medication — plenty of people use
both medication and behavioral strategies together, and that's a decision to make with a doctor,
not a blog post. What follows are structural, non-medication strategies for the hours you need
to focus without it, whether that's by choice, by circumstance, or alongside a prescription.

## Work with your attention pattern, not against it

ADHD attention isn't uniformly weak — it's inconsistent, and it can also lock onto something
intensely (hyperfocus) under the right conditions. The goal isn't to force flat, constant
attention like a neurotypical strategy might assume. It's to engineer more of the conditions that
tend to produce your good focus windows, and to protect work from the conditions that reliably
break it.

### Time-box instead of open-ending a task

"Work on this for 25 minutes" is easier to start and sustain than "work on this until it's done,"
because it removes the open-ended commitment that makes tasks feel heavier than they are — the
same mechanism covered in
[How to Start a Task You're Dreading (ADHD)](/blog/how-to-start-a-task-you-are-dreading-adhd).
Short, timed blocks with real breaks between them tend to outperform long, unstructured stretches.

### Remove switching costs before you start, not during

Close every tab and app you don't need for this specific block, before you start the timer — not
"after this one more check." Once a session is running, every switch has a real cost: getting
back to where you were takes real, disproportionate effort for an ADHD brain compared to a
neurotypical one, so the cheapest time to prevent switching is before it happens, not during.

### Use body doubling for focus blocks, not just chores

The same effect covered in
[Body Doubling Apps for ADHD](/blog/body-doubling-apps-for-adhd) applies directly to work focus
sessions — a co-working call or shared virtual room can lower the barrier to starting and staying
on a focus block, not just to starting household tasks.

### Match hard tasks to your actual good hours

Track, loosely, when your focus tends to be best over a week or two — most people have a
consistent pattern once they look for it. Schedule the tasks that need real attention into that
window, and push low-stakes, low-focus tasks (email, admin) into the hours where focus is
typically worse instead of fighting your own pattern.

### Make the next step visible before a break

Before stepping away, write down the literal next action ("open the file and start editing
paragraph 3," not "keep working on the report"). Coming back to a vague task restarts the
initiation barrier from scratch; coming back to a specific, tiny next step doesn't.

## When this isn't enough

If none of the above meaningfully helps and work is consistently difficult to manage, that's
useful information to bring to a doctor — these strategies are a complement to professional care,
not a substitute for a conversation with one if things aren't working.
