Most cleaning schedules assume a level, predictable amount of motivation every day. ADHD
motivation doesn't work that way — it's inconsistent, task-specific, and largely indifferent to
how important the task actually is. A schedule that doesn't account for that gets abandoned
within a week, and the abandonment itself often becomes another source of shame that makes
starting again harder.

## Why "clean the whole house Saturday" doesn't work

A single big cleaning day requires sustained motivation across many different task types
(dishes, laundry, floors, bathroom) back to back. If motivation drops after the first task —
which is common — the whole day's plan collapses, and because it was one block, there's no
partial win to point to. Smaller, type-separated tasks fail independently instead of together.

## The structure that holds up better

### Sort tasks by category, not by day

Group tasks by *type of attention* they need, not by which room they're in:

- **Low-energy, can do while distracted:** wiping counters, loading the dishwasher, quick tidy
- **Medium-energy, needs some focus:** laundry start-to-finish, vacuuming a room
- **High-energy, needs a clear head:** deep bathroom clean, organizing a cluttered space

### Match tasks to your actual energy, not the calendar

Instead of assigning "bathroom = Tuesday," keep a short list of tasks in each energy category and
pick from whichever list matches how you feel that day. This removes the failure mode where a
high-energy task lands on a low-energy day and just doesn't happen.

### Use a visible tracker, not a mental one

A printed weekly grid where you physically mark a task done works better than a mental list —
the same principle covered in
[ADHD Morning Routine for People Who Hate Mornings](/blog/adhd-morning-routine-for-people-who-hate-mornings).
Seeing checked boxes accumulate is also a concrete, visible reward that a mental checklist can't
provide.

### Build in a "minimum viable clean"

For each category, define the smallest version that still counts. "Deep clean the bathroom" and
"wipe the sink and toilet seat" are both valid entries — on a low-energy day, doing the small
version and marking it done keeps the system alive instead of skipping it entirely.

## What to avoid

- **Daily rigid schedules** ("Monday = kitchen, Tuesday = bathroom") — one missed day breaks the
  whole week's logic.
- **All-or-nothing tasks** with no smaller version defined in advance — decision-making in the
  moment about how to scale a task down is exactly the kind of extra step that stalls execution.
- **Schedules with no visible progress tracker** — out of sight really is out of mind here.
