Most fidget tool marketing is aimed at kids, which creates a real problem for ADHD adults: the
most visible options are bright, loud (clicky), and obviously a toy — not something you can use
in a meeting or an open office without drawing attention. The tools exist for adults too; they're
just harder to find because they're not what gets marketed loudest.

## Why fidgeting helps (briefly)

The working theory is that fidgeting provides a low-level, secondary channel of stimulation that
can help sustain attention on a primary task — for some ADHD brains, having *some* physical
outlet for restlessness frees up more attention for the actual task at hand, rather than
competing with it. It doesn't work the same way for everyone, and it's worth testing cheaply
before buying several options.

## What to look for as an adult

### Silent operation

Clicking, snapping fidget toys are the most common and the least usable in a professional
setting. Look specifically for tools described as "silent" — textured surfaces, smooth sliders,
soft-touch materials — rather than anything with a mechanical click.

### One-handed use

If you need a tool while also typing, writing, or holding a phone, it needs to work fully
one-handed and without looking at it. Test this mentally against your actual daily use before
buying — a tool that requires two hands or visual attention defeats the purpose in a meeting.

### Discreet size and material

Something pocket-sized in matte metal, wood, or soft silicone reads as a normal object on a desk
rather than a toy. This matters less at home, but matters a lot in a shared office.

### Texture variety, tested cheaply first

Different people respond to different textures (smooth, ridged, squishy, cool-to-the-touch) —
there's no universal "best" material. A cheap multi-texture starter set is a better first
purchase than committing to one premium tool before knowing what you actually respond to.

## Categories worth knowing

- **Slider/glider tools** — smooth, silent, good for meetings
- **Textured worry stones or discs** — pocket-sized, purely tactile, no moving parts to make noise
- **Soft squeeze tools** — good for desk use, less discreet in your hand in public
- **Chewable jewelry** (necklaces, bracelets) — a different sensory channel entirely, worth
  knowing about if hand-fidgeting isn't your pattern — some people's restlessness is more
  oral than tactile

## The honest limitation

Fidget tools address restlessness, not task initiation or time-blindness — they're a
complement to the systems covered in
[How to Start a Task You're Dreading (ADHD)](/blog/how-to-start-a-task-you-are-dreading-adhd),
not a replacement for them.
