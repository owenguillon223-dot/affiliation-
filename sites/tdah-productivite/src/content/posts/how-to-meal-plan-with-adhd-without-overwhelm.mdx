Standard meal planning advice asks you to decide, on one day, every meal for the next seven —
which means making dozens of small decisions in one sitting, for a version of you a week away
whose energy, mood, and schedule you can't actually predict yet. That's a lot of decision-making
concentrated in one place, and it's a big part of why meal planning is one of the most commonly
abandoned household systems.

## The core problem: planning too far ahead of your actual state

By Wednesday, "Wednesday's planned dinner" was decided by a version of you from Sunday who didn't
know Wednesday would be exhausting. The plan and your actual capacity drift apart, and once they
drift far enough, the whole system gets abandoned rather than adjusted.

## A structure that drifts less

### Plan a rotation, not a calendar

Instead of assigning specific meals to specific days, keep a short list of 8-10 meals you
actually like and can make without much thought, and pick from that list based on how you feel
each day — not based on what a past version of you assigned to today. This removes the mismatch
between planned-you and actual-you entirely, because there's no fixed schedule to drift from.

### Sort the rotation by effort, like the cleaning schedule

The same energy-matching idea from
[ADHD-Friendly Cleaning Schedule](/blog/adhd-friendly-cleaning-schedule) applies here directly:

- **Low-effort:** meals that need close to zero real cooking (assembled, not prepared)
- **Medium-effort:** one-pan or one-pot meals, minimal steps
- **Higher-effort:** meals for days you actually have the energy and want to cook

On a bad day, picking from the low-effort list and still eating a real meal is a win — not a
failure to follow "the plan."

### Buy for the rotation, not for a week

Keep the ingredients for your full rotation stocked as a standing baseline, rather than buying
fresh for a specific week's plan. This means a bad, low-energy week doesn't mean an empty fridge
— whatever energy shows up that day, the ingredients for something in the rotation are already
there.

### Make the list visible, not remembered

Same principle as everywhere else on this site: a printed or posted list of your rotation options
beats trying to remember what's in it while standing in front of an open fridge, tired, at 6pm —
the worst possible moment to be relying on memory.

## What to drop entirely

- Pinterest-style themed weekly meal plans with a different, novel recipe every night — high
  decision cost, low resilience to a bad day
- Anything that requires prep the exact day before or it "won't work" — too fragile against a
  missed day
- Tracking or logging every meal in detail — useful for some goals, but an extra maintenance
  burden most ADHD meal-planning systems don't actually need to succeed
