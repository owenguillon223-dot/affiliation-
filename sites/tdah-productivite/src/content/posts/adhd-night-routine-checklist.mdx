If you've read
[ADHD Morning Routine for People Who Hate Mornings](/blog/adhd-morning-routine-for-people-who-hate-mornings),
you already know the core idea: decisions made the night before don't have to be made again,
half-asleep, under time pressure, the next day. This is the other half of that system.

## Why nights are actually the easier time to plan

By evening, most of the day's decisions are already behind you — there's less competing for
attention than first thing in the morning, when everything (the day ahead, notifications, whether
you're already running late) is happening at once. That makes the evening a genuinely better time
to make small decisions for tomorrow, not just a nice-to-have habit.

## The checklist

Keep this short — a long night routine is exactly as likely to get skipped as a long morning one.

1. **Clothes laid out** — including anything weather- or context-specific (gym clothes, an
   umbrella by the door), so tomorrow-morning-you doesn't have to think about it
2. **Bag packed** — keys, wallet, anything you need for the day, by the door, not "somewhere
   you'll remember"
3. **Tomorrow's first task written down, visibly** — not the whole day, just the one thing you
   want to be true you did by 10am
4. **Phone charging outside the bedroom** — removes the most common morning time-sink before it
   can happen
5. **One thing tidied, not everything** — pick the surface that will bother you most in the
   morning if it's still messy, and just do that one

## Where this tends to break down

The most common failure isn't forgetting the routine exists — it's starting it too late, after
you're already tired, at which point every step feels heavier than it is. If that's a pattern for
you, anchor the routine to something that already happens reliably and earlier than you think —
right after dinner, rather than "before bed," which tends to arrive later than planned. This is
the same anchoring principle covered in the morning routine article, applied in reverse.

## Keep it visible, not mental

A printed checklist by the door or on the fridge outperforms a mental list for the same reason
covered in the morning and cleaning-schedule articles: an external, visible system doesn't have
to compete with every other thought in your head for attention.
