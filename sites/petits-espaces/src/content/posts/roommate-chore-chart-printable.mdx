Most roommate chore agreements fail not because nobody agreed to a split, but because the
agreement was verbal, made once, and never revisited — which means disagreements about fairness
have no shared reference point to settle them.

## Write it down, even if it feels unnecessary at first

A written chart — even a simple one taped to the fridge — does something a verbal agreement
can't: it makes the division of labor visible and checkable, which prevents the most common
roommate conflict pattern of each person quietly believing they're doing more than their share.

## Split by task type, not by strict 50/50 time

Chores aren't equally unpleasant or equally time-consuming, and a rigid time-based split ignores
that. A workable approach: list every recurring task, rate roughly how unpleasant or effortful
each one is, and balance the *total effort*, not the *number of tasks* — two easy tasks
shouldn't outweigh one genuinely dreaded one.

## Rotate tasks that nobody wants permanently

For the least popular chores (deep bathroom cleaning is the most commonly cited example),
rotating on a set schedule rather than assigning permanently prevents resentment from building
around one person always doing the worst job.

## Build in a review point

Set a specific, low-stakes moment — monthly is common — to revisit whether the split still feels
fair, rather than letting frustration build silently until it becomes a bigger conflict. A chart
that's never revisited stops reflecting reality within a few weeks as schedules and circumstances
change.

## Separate shared chores from personal space

This chart should cover shared spaces and shared responsibilities only (kitchen, bathroom,
common areas, shared bills like household supplies) — personal bedrooms and individual
responsibilities don't belong on a shared chart and tend to create unnecessary friction if
included.
